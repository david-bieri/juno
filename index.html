JUNO: A Trilemma Framework
for GSE Reform Analytics

No reform mechanism can simultaneously achieve market discipline, systemic stability, and implementation simplicity. More than a policy choice problem — a temporal trap where prioritizing one objective initiates processes that eventually eliminate access to all three.

A browser-native implementation of the GSE reform trilemma framework: fourteen policy options evaluated across mechanism design criteria, regulatory complexity, and stakeholder consensus; three agent-based simulations revealing the structural constraints on each trilemma vertex; and a temporal trap model demonstrating why reform became structurally impossible at year 12 of the conservatorship. No build step. No server. No dependencies.

Scoring & Stakeholder Reranking

Complexity
The Inverse Relationship
Scatter plot of all 14 options: regulatory complexity score (x-axis) against stakeholder consensus score (y-axis). The inverse relationship — administratively feasible options score 20–30 and achieve highest consensus; legislatively superior options score 70–80 and remain politically stalled — is the tool's core empirical finding. Heat map of all 14 options graded across four mechanism design criteria: incentive compatibility, information revelation, participation constraints, and efficiency. The central tension: FMIC grades A across all dimensions but is unimplementable; the Hybrid Utility-Competition Model grades C+ but is the most robust implementable option.

How it works
Methodology

The Trilemma

No GSE reform mechanism can simultaneously achieve market discipline (competition, price discovery, private risk-bearing), systemic stability (30-year FRM availability, TBA market liquidity, catastrophic government backstop), and implementation simplicity (administrative feasibility, short timelines, minimal inter-agency coordination). Policymakers must sacrifice at least one vertex. But the trilemma is not a static choice: three simulations reveal it is a temporal trap where prioritizing one vertex initiates processes that eventually eliminate access to all three.

Simulation 1 — Institutional Complementarities (Tab 08)

The GSE structure is embedded in a six-institution complementarity network. System fitness is the sum of pairwise alignment terms. Attempting isolated reform decreases system fitness by 23%, limiting effectiveness to 28% of target. The complementarity matrix κ_ij strengthens during conservatorship — by year 17, even comprehensive reform faces critical resistance.

Fitness = Σᵢ Σⱼ κᵢⱼ × (1 − |sᵢ − sⱼ|) Δsᵢ(t) = αᵢ × ∂Fitness/∂sᵢ − cᵢ × (sᵢ − sᵢ,initial) κᵢⱼ(t) = κᵢⱼ(0) × (1 + 0.03t) [time-varying strengthening]

Simulation 2 — Coordination Game (Tab 07)

GSE reform is modeled as an N-player coordination game. Each agency chooses effort to maximize its own payoff given others' choices. Three agencies (HUD, SEC, CFPB) have negative benefit parameters and rationally shirk. Success probability scales exponentially with N — each additional required agency reduces success by approximately 10 percentage points.

P(success) ≈ p^N where p ≈ 0.85 (mean coordination probability) N=2 (Administrative): 85% success N=9 (Multiple Guarantors): 23% success N=12 (FMIC): 12% success

Simulation 3 — Lock-In Accumulation (Tab 06)

Lock-in strength L(t) aggregates four mechanisms weighted by their contribution to path dependence. Reform cost grows with L(t). The political feasibility limit is fixed at 150 arbitrary units. When reform cost exceeds this limit — at year 12 of conservatorship, approximately 2020 — reform becomes structurally impossible regardless of political will.

L(t) = α·Learning(t) + β·Coordination(t) + γ·Expectations(t) + δ·Sunk_costs(t) [weights: α=1.5, β=2.0 (network externalities dominant), γ=1.8, δ=1.2] Reform_cost(t) = 50 × [1 + 1.5 × L(t)] Critical threshold: Reform_cost(t*) = 150 → t* ≈ year 12 (2020) Current (year 17): Reform_cost = 1,090 → 7.3× feasibility limit

Data and Scoring

All mechanism design grades, regulatory complexity scores, and consensus scores are computed outputs from the analytical framework underlying Bieri (2026), manuscript in preparation for Socio-Economic Review. Scores are fixed; stakeholder weighting sliders apply five pre-specified weight vectors to the fixed underlying scores. Simulation 1 results are pre-computed Python outputs (Radau stiff ODE solver). Simulations 2 and 3 run live in the browser using closed-form JS implementations.
